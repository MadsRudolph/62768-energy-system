\subsection{Simulation and MPPT Control Behavior}

The simulation does not represent a classical closed-loop control system that regulates the output to a fixed reference value. Instead, it implements a Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) algorithm based on the Perturb and Observe method.\\
\\
In this approach, the controller does not aim to track a predefined voltage or current. Instead, it continuously adjusts the PWM duty cycle in order to find the operating point that maximizes the power output of the PV module.\\
\\
This is fundamentally different from traditional PID control, where the objective is to minimize the error between a reference value and the measured output. In the MPPT system, the objective is to maximize the power, meaning that the system performs an optimization rather than reference tracking.\\
\\
A key component in the simulation is the buck converter. The buck converter enables control of the operating point by adjusting the effective load seen by the PV module. By changing the PWM duty cycle, the controller changes the current drawn from the PV module, which shifts the operating point along the I--V curve.\\
\\
In the experimental setup, the PV module was connected directly to a resistive load. In this case, the operating point is fixed by the load, and no active control is possible. The measurements were therefore used to determine the I/V and P/V characteristics and to identify the maximum power point.\\
\\
In contrast, the simulation includes a buck converter, which acts as a controllable interface between the PV module and the load. This allows the MPPTalgorithm to dynamically adjust the operating point and track the maximum power point.\\
\\
For this reason, the simulation results are not expected to match the measured voltage, current, and power values directly. The purpose of the simulation is instead to demonstrate that the control strategy works and that the system is able to converge to a stable operating point where the power is maximum

\subsection{Drive Circuit}
\label{sec:DriveCircuit}



\noindent
The driver circuit is used to control the DC motor that drives the three-phase generator in the energy system. The motor speed is controlled by applying a PWM signal to the driver circuit. By changing the PWM duty cycle, the average voltage applied to the motor changes, which makes it possible to regulate the generator speed and therefore the generated voltage.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
In this project, the driver circuit consists of a CNY17-3 optocoupler, an IR2110 gate driver, and an IRF540N MOSFET. The circuit is designed to allow a low-voltage microcontroller signal to control a higher-power motor circuit. The microcontroller generates the PWM signal, while the MOSFET switches the motor current on and off.

\vspace{0.5cm}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{report/images/DriveCircuit/Drivecircuit-KiCad.png}
\caption{KiCad schematic of the motor driver circuit used in the project. - Lecture 2, Exp 3A}
\label{fig:driver_circuit_KiCad}
\end{figure}

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
The PWM signal from the microcontroller is first sent through the CNY17-3 optocoupler. The purpose of the optocoupler is to provide galvanic isolation between the low-voltage control side and the motor power side. This is important because the motor and generator can create electrical noise, voltage spikes, and ground potential differences. The optocoupler helps protect the microcontroller from these disturbances.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
The input side of the CNY17-3 contains an internal LED. A series resistor is used to limit the current through this LED and prevent damage. When the microcontroller sends a PWM signal, the LED inside the optocoupler turns on and off. On the output side of the optocoupler, this signal is transferred optically and used as the input signal for the IR2110 gate driver.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
The IR2110 is used as a MOSFET gate driver. A microcontroller output pin cannot normally supply enough current to charge and discharge the MOSFET gate quickly. Therefore, the IR2110 is used to amplify the control signal and provide a stronger gate drive signal to the MOSFET. This allows the MOSFET to switch faster and more reliably.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
In this circuit, the IR2110 is mainly used to drive the low-side MOSFET. The MOSFET used is an IRF540N N-channel MOSFET. The motor is connected between the positive supply and the drain of the MOSFET, while the source of the MOSFET is connected to ground. When the PWM signal turns the MOSFET on, current flows through the motor and the MOSFET to ground. When the MOSFET turns off, the motor current is interrupted.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
A flyback diode is placed across the motor. This diode is important because the motor is an inductive load. When the MOSFET turns off, the motor inductance tries to keep the current flowing, which can create a large voltage spike. The flyback diode gives this current a safe path and helps protect the MOSFET from overvoltage.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
The circuit also includes resistors around the MOSFET gate. These resistors help control the switching behaviour of the MOSFET. A gate resistor limits the peak gate current and reduces ringing during switching. A gate-to-source resistor is also used to make sure the MOSFET stays off when no valid gate signal is present. This prevents unintended switching of the motor.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
Decoupling capacitors are placed close to the IR2110 supply pins. These capacitors help stabilize the driver supply voltage and reduce the effect of switching noise. This is important because the MOSFET gate driver needs a stable supply voltage to operate reliably.

\subsubsection*{Reasoning for choice of components}

\noindent
The following calculations justify the most important component values used in the motor driver circuit. Since the function of the optocoupler, gate driver, MOSFET, flyback diode, and decoupling capacitors has already been explained above, this section mainly focuses on the selected resistor values and switching considerations.

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
\textbf{Optocoupler LED series resistor}

\noindent
The input LED of the CNY17-3 optocoupler must be driven with a controlled current. To achieve reliable switching while still protecting the microcontroller output and the optocoupler LED, the LED current is chosen as:

\[
I_F = 10~\text{mA}
\]

\noindent
The resistor value can be calculated as:

\[
R = \frac{V_{\text{MCU}} - V_F}{I_F}
\]

\noindent
Using \(V_{\text{MCU}} = 3.3~\text{V}\), \(V_F = 1.3~\text{V}\), and \(I_F = 10~\text{mA}\), the resistor value becomes:

\[
R = \frac{3.3~\text{V} - 1.3~\text{V}}{0.01~\text{A}}
\]

\[
R = 200~\Omega
\]

\noindent
A \(200~\Omega\) resistor is therefore selected. This gives approximately:

\[
I_F = \frac{V_{\text{MCU}} - V_F}{R}
\]

\[
I_F = \frac{3.3~\text{V} - 1.3~\text{V}}{200~\Omega}
\]

\[
I_F = 10~\text{mA}
\]

\noindent
This current is suitable for the CNY17-3 and ensures that the optocoupler can transfer the PWM signal reliably.

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
\textbf{Optocoupler output resistor}

\noindent
On the output side of the CNY17-3, a \(1~\text{k}\Omega\) resistor is used to make sure that the IR2110 input has a defined logic level. This prevents the gate driver input from floating when the optocoupler is not conducting. The CNY17-3 is chosen because its current transfer ratio gives better margin when transferring the PWM signal compared with lower CTR optocoupler versions.

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
\textbf{MOSFET gate resistor}

\noindent
The gate resistor is selected to control how quickly the MOSFET gate is charged and discharged. The IRF540N has a total gate charge of approximately:

\[
Q_g = 71~\text{nC}
\]

\noindent
The required gate current can be estimated as:

\[
I_g = \frac{Q_g}{t_{sw}}
\]

\noindent
Using \(Q_g = 71~\text{nC}\) and an assumed switching time of \(t_{sw} = 200~\text{ns}\), the gate current becomes:

\[
I_g = \frac{71~\text{nC}}{200~\text{ns}}
\]

\[
I_g \approx 0.355~\text{A}
\]

\noindent
The theoretical gate resistance can then be estimated as:

\[
R_g = \frac{V_{GS}}{I_g}
\]

\noindent
Using \(V_{GS} = 15~\text{V}\) and \(I_g = 0.355~\text{A}\), the gate resistance becomes:

\[
R_g = \frac{15~\text{V}}{0.355~\text{A}}
\]

\[
R_g \approx 42~\Omega
\]

\noindent
In the practical circuit, a smaller gate resistor is used to obtain faster switching and reduce switching losses. The resistor still helps limit the peak gate current and reduces ringing in the gate signal.

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
\textbf{Decoupling capacitors}

\noindent
A (100~\text{nF}) capacitor is placed close to the IR2110 supply pins to reduce high-frequency noise. A larger capacitor is also used on the supply rail to support the driver during switching. These capacitors help keep the gate-driver supply stable when the MOSFET switches the motor current.

\vspace{0.5cm}

\noindent
Overall, the chosen component values allow the PWM signal to be transferred safely through the CNY17-3, while the IR2110 and IRF540N can switch the motor current reliably. The selected resistor and capacitor values help protect the circuit, reduce noise, and improve switching stability.

\noindent
The driver circuit is used as part of the generator control system. By changing the PWM duty cycle, the controller can adjust the speed of the DC motor. A higher duty cycle gives a higher average motor voltage, which increases the motor speed and generator output voltage. A lower duty cycle reduces the motor voltage, which lowers the generator speed and output voltage.

\subsection*{PID Control of the Motor}
A PID controller is used to regulate the output voltage of the motor-generator system. 
The system dynamics are characterized by a dominant pole at approximately 37 rad/s and an additional pole at 7 rad/s. 
A reference voltage \(V_{ref}\) is defined as the desired output level, and the controller adjusts the system such that the measured output voltage follows this reference.
\noindent
The controller compares the measured output voltage with the reference voltage, and the difference between these is defined as the error:

\[
e(t) = V_{ref} - V_{out}
\]
\noindent
This error signal is used as the input to the digital PID controller, which generates a control signal based on proportional, integral, and derivative actions. 
The controller output determines the PWM duty cycle, which is sent through the drive circuit to control the DC motor.
\noindent
The PID parameters (\(K_p\), \(K_i\), and \(K_d\)) are selected based on the system dynamics. 
The dominant time constant \(\tau_1 \approx 0.027\) s provides a guideline for the tuning.
\noindent
An initial estimate of the integral gain can be found as:

\[
K_i \approx \frac{K_p}{\tau_1}
\]
\noindent
The derivative gain can be approximated as:

\[
K_d \approx K_p \cdot \tau_1
\]
\noindent
In practice, however, the theoretical values obtained from these relations are rarely optimal for the real system. 
This is because the physical system includes non-ideal effects such as friction, nonlinearities, delays, and component tolerances, which are not fully captured in the simplified model.
\noindent
The final PID parameters were experimentally selected as:

\[
K_p = 20, \quad K_i = 2, \quad K_d = 0.6
\]
\noindent
The proportional gain \(K_p\) was chosen through iterative tuning. 
The tuning process started with a small value of \(K_p\), which was gradually increased while observing the system response. 
Increasing \(K_p\) improves the response speed of the system, but too high values result in oscillations. 
The selected value \(K_p = 20\) was found to provide a fast response without causing instability.
\noindent
The integral gain \(K_i = 2\) was chosen to eliminate steady-state error. 
A larger value of \(K_i\) can lead to oscillatory behaviour, so a moderate value was selected to ensure stable convergence to the reference.
\noindent
The derivative gain \(K_d = 0.6\) was introduced to provide damping of oscillations. 
Since the system is already relatively fast, only a small derivative term is required. 
The derivative action reduces overshoot and improves stability.
\noindent
Overall, the selected PID parameters represent a compromise between theoretical predictions and practical performance. 
The final values were obtained through iterative tuning based on simulation and experimental testing, where they provided the most stable and efficient operation of the system.
